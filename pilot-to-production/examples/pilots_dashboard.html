The number that changes the conversation: naive CAC counts only the pilots you won — $109,630. True CAC divides all pilot cost by the wins: $580,489, or 5.3×. The lost and stalled pilots are not overhead; they are the cost of acquiring the customers you did win. At an average $191,585 ACV and 68% deployment gross margin, that CAC takes 53 months of gross profit to repay (a revenue-basis figure would read 36 months and overstate the payback) — which is the real conclusion on this page: pilot-heavy acquisition is only solvent on multi-year terms. Against a three-year deployment contract the same CAC is 101% of TCV, which is workable. Against a one-year contract it is not. In robotics, contract term is a financial lever every bit as important as price, and it is usually negotiated by people who have never seen this number.

And the counterfactual is the actionable half: had only the qualified pilots been run — named sponsor plus written success criteria — true CAC would be $170,357, or 31% of three-year TCV against 101% actual. The unqualified pilots are not underperforming; they are the entire reason the acquisition motion does not pay for itself.
