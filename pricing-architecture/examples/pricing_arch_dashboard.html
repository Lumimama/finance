Question 1 — Pricing: revenue by month, demand held identical

Same robots, same tasks, same customers — only the pricing logic differs, so the spread between these lines isolates the pricing architecture and nothing else. On identical units, subscription earns most because it charges most per robot; the royalty rate billed on the same base earns $34.8M against $49.0M. As a price, royalty is simply worse.

Question 2 — Distribution: royalty + OEM reach

This is a different question, and it is not a pricing choice. Embedding in OEM production reaches 2.4× the units you could sell direct, so demand is deliberately not held constant here. On that basis royalty earns $83.60M against $48.98M for subscription — it wins on reach, not on rate.

And that is where the risk appears. The same royalty rate carries 32% concentration when billed direct and 61% when it flows through OEM partners who own the customer relationship, the hardware roadmap, and therefore the renewal. Concentration is a consequence of the distribution decision, not the pricing one — which is exactly why the two questions have to be answered separately.

Metering aligns price with delivered value and is the least forecastable — 5.3% month-over-month volatility against 0.1% for subscription. That alignment is bought with forecast error, and forecast error at a company still proving its plan to a board is expensive in ways the revenue line doesn't show.

Royalty is not a pricing choice. It wins revenue here only because it reaches 2.4× the units — it is a go-to-market bet, trading the customer relationship for reach. Comparing it to the direct models on revenue alone is a category error.

Among the direct models, hybrid sits between the other two on both axes: $34.8M revenue at 3.4% volatility.
What this model cannot answer. Demand here is exogenous and identical across all four architectures — that is what makes the comparison clean, and it is also the limitation. It cannot value the two things that actually push companies toward hybrid: the expansion motion, where revenue grows with usage without a renegotiation, and the churn avoided by not overcharging light users. On the axes this model can measure, plain per-robot subscription wins among the direct options. Anyone reading that as "therefore price per robot" has taken the model past what it supports — which is worth saying out loud on the page rather than in a footnote.
